# Conferences and Journals

### The five venues that matter most

If you're submitting research or want to track the field, these are the **tier-1 robotics venues** (in rough order of selectivity/impact):

| Venue | Type | What it's known for |
|---|---|---|
| **RSS** - Robotics: Science and Systems | Conference (annual) | Most selective robotics conference; deeply theoretical + systems work. Single-track. |
| **CoRL** - Conference on Robot Learning | Conference (annual) | The home of robot learning, imitation, RL, foundation models. Growing fast since 2017. |
| **ICRA** - IEEE Int'l Conf. on Robotics and Automation | Conference (annual, spring) | Largest robotics conference; broad scope; IEEE RAS flagship. |
| **IROS** - IEEE/RSJ Int'l Conf. on Intelligent Robots and Systems | Conference (annual, fall) | Second largest; emphasis on intelligent systems. |
| **Humanoids** - IEEE-RAS Int'l Conf. on Humanoid Robots | Conference (annual) | Specialized; the canonical humanoid venue. |

This ordering is about selectivity and prestige, not about where you should submit first. RSS runs single-track by design, which caps how many papers it can accept regardless of quality - every accepted talk is a genuine event. CoRL is younger and narrower (robot learning specifically) but has become the venue where foundation-model-for-robotics work lands. ICRA and IROS are an order of magnitude larger, cover the entire field from mechanism design to swarm coordination, and are where most working roboticists publish most of their papers - not because the bar is low, but because the scope is wide enough that a much larger fraction of solid, less flashy work has a home there.

**Worth also tracking:** ICAR (Int'l Conf. on Advanced Robotics), ISRR (Int'l Symp. on Robotics Research, every 2 years - invite-only), Field & Service Robotics (FSR, field/outdoor and service robotics), RoboCup Symposium (the academic track that sits alongside the RoboCup competitions), ROSCon (community/dev, see below), and ICLR/NeurIPS/ICML/CVPR (mainstream ML and vision venues that now carry growing robot-learning or robot-vision tracks as foundation models touch robotics).

**Workshops at ICRA, IROS, RSS, CoRL** are often where state-of-the-art is presented before journal or main-track publication. Skim the workshop lists every year - they're frequently more current than the main proceedings.

**arXiv `cs.RO`** is where most papers land before and/or after formal publication - see below for why that matters more in robotics than in most fields. RSS announcements: [robotics-worldwide mailing list](https://www.lists.kit.edu/sympa/info/robotics-worldwide).

### Using conferences to actually build a career

Knowing that RSS is prestigious doesn't tell you what to do with that fact as a student or a career-switcher. The venues above serve genuinely different purposes depending on where you are.

**Where to aim your first paper.** Don't aim your first submission at RSS or CoRL - the bar is high and the single-track/high-selectivity format means a rough first paper competes directly against polished ones from established labs. A workshop paper at ICRA, IROS, RSS, or CoRL is the realistic on-ramp: lower friction, faster feedback, and a legitimate line on a CV or PhD application. A main-track paper at ICRA or IROS is the next realistic step once you have a full study, not just an idea. RSS/CoRL become realistic once you have a track record or a genuinely strong result.

**Workshop paper vs. main-track paper - know the difference.** A workshop submission is shorter, reviewed faster and less rigorously, and attached to a niche topic session at a big conference. It is a real venue for early feedback and visibility, but it does not carry the same weight as a main-track paper for hiring committees or admissions - main-track papers go through the full multi-reviewer (often multi-round) process that the field treats as the actual publication record. Read both, but don't present one as the other on a CV.

**ROSCon is a different kind of value entirely.** It has no peer-reviewed academic proceedings in the RSS/ICRA sense, so it's not a place to build a publication record. Its value is that it's where the people who maintain the ROS packages you depend on actually show up, where companies recruit engineers rather than researchers, and where "I shipped X and it runs on Y robots" matters more than a citation count. If your goal is an industry robotics engineering role rather than a research career, ROSCon is arguably more useful to attend than RSS. I'd rather hire someone who can point to a maintained ROS package with real users than someone with a workshop paper nobody's read. See [Getting Hired](getting-hired.md) for how any of this converts into an actual job.

**arXiv `cs.RO` exists because the field outruns its own review cycles.** A conference review cycle runs 4-9 months from submission to presentation; robot learning in particular moves faster than that. By the time a CoRL paper is presented, two or three follow-ups are often already on arXiv. People who work in the field track `cs.RO` directly rather than waiting for proceedings - treat conference acceptance as a lagging quality signal (it tells you a result held up to review), not as the channel where you find out what's new.

If you're trying to work out which labs actually publish at which of these venues before you apply anywhere, cross-reference [Leading Institutes](leading-institutes.md).
